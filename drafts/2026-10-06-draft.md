## The Challenge

A U.S. nonprofit community hospital with about 490 registered nurses (RNs) loses roughly 86 nurses each year. At the 2025 national staff RN turnover rate of 17.6%, and with each departure costing an average of $60,090 to replace, turnover costs the hospital about $5.2 million annually in recruitment, onboarding, overtime, and temporary staffing (NSI, 2026). Nurse staffing levels also affect patient outcomes. Aiken et al. (2002) found that each additional patient assigned to a nurse was associated with 7% higher odds of death within 30 days of admission. When a nurse leaves, the position stays open until a replacement is hired and trained, which can strain staffing. This paper does not measure that effect, so it is context for the decision rather than part of the ROI.

This paper is written for the hospital's chief financial officer (CFO), who must decide whether a nurse residency program for newly graduated RNs is worth funding. The hospital is sized to match the average hospital reported by NSI ($294,976 per point of turnover ÷ $60,090 per departure ≈ 490 RNs). To determine when the program saves more than it costs, the analysis examines three links: (1) whether RN supply is inelastic, (2) what turnover costs the hospital, and (3) whether the residency program pays for itself. Each link is tested against criteria set before the data were analyzed.

## Is RN Supply Inelastic?

One reason hospitals invest in retention is the belief that increasing wages alone will not solve nursing shortages. If RN supply is inelastic, higher pay brings relatively few additional nurses into the workforce, so keeping current nurses matters more.

To test this idea, I calculated an observed employment-wage ratio by dividing the percentage change in RN employment by the percentage change in the real median RN wage between May 2015 and May 2019 (BLS OEWS data adjusted using CPI-U). A ratio below 1.0 would be consistent with inelastic supply. Instead, RN employment increased by 8.6% while the real median wage increased by only 0.68%, producing a ratio of approximately 12.7. Every year-to-year change during the period was also above 1.0 (Figure 1).

By the pre-set threshold, this link failed. The more likely explanation is that supply and demand shifted together: demand grew with an aging population and more complex patient needs, while supply grew as nursing schools expanded. Because this measure cannot separate the two shifts, it cannot show whether RN supply is elastic or inelastic.

For the CFO, this result does not change the decision: turnover remains costly, and the question is whether reducing it saves more than it costs.

## What Turnover Costs This Hospital

NSI reports staff RN turnover rates of 18.4% in 2023, 16.4% in 2024, and 17.6% in 2025 (NSI, 2026). Turnover fell 0.8 percentage points, so the pre-set test (a rise of at least one point) was not met. However, the case for a residency program depends on the level of turnover, not its trend, and at 17.6% the hospital still faces the $5.2 million annual cost described above.

## Does the Residency Program Pay?

The program evaluated is the new-graduate transition-to-practice residency studied by Silvestre et al. (2017). In a multisite study of 1,032 new graduate nurses across 70 hospitals, turnover after 12 months was 15.5% for nurses in the residency program compared with 26.8% for those who did not participate, a reduction of 11.3 percentage points. The program cost $3,908 per new graduate in 2013 dollars, including ongoing and one-time development costs. I chose it because it is one of the few retention strategies with both a published cost and a measured turnover effect.

I applied the study's results to the case hospital, assuming 15 new-graduate hires per year (the study's average). An 11.3 percentage-point reduction in turnover would prevent about 1.7 departures annually. At $60,090 per departure, that avoids about $101,853 in turnover costs. This analysis assumes that turnover among newly graduated nurses costs the same to replace as the average staff RN reported by NSI (2026). That may not be exact because new graduates can have different orientation and training costs than experienced nurses. If replacement costs are lower for new graduates, the estimated ROI of 1.26 would decrease. If replacement costs are higher, the ROI would increase. Therefore, the ROI should be viewed as an estimate rather than a precise prediction. After adjusting program costs to 2025 dollars using CPI-U, the program would cost about $81,012 in total, or $66,024 if the hospital adopted an existing program and paid only ongoing costs.

*Table 1. Return on investment (avoided turnover cost ÷ program cost)*

| Cost basis | Full effect | Half effect | Break-even share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full cost ($81,012) | 1.26 | 0.63 | ~80% |
| Ongoing cost only ($66,024) | 1.54 | 0.77 | ~65% |

At the published effect the program pays for itself, but at half the effect the ROI falls below break-even on both cost bases. Break-even requires about 80% of the published effect on full cost, or about 65% on ongoing cost only (Figure 2). This is a cost-benefit test built on the logic of marginal analysis: the program is worth funding only where the turnover cost it avoids at least equals its cost. The decision therefore depends less on the published average than on whether the hospital can implement the program well enough to match it.

## Recommendation to the CFO

Based on these results, I recommend funding the nurse residency program only if the hospital can reasonably expect to achieve most of the turnover reduction reported by Silvestre et al. (2017). For this hospital, the program breaks even if it achieves about 80% of the published effect, or about 65% if the hospital adopts an existing program and pays only ongoing costs. Adopting an established program and implementing it as designed improves the chances of success. The hospital should track first-year new-graduate turnover and review results after 12 months. If results fall below the break-even threshold, the program should be reevaluated before additional resources are committed.

Even if successful, the program would prevent only about two of the hospital's roughly 86 annual RN departures because it affects only the 15 new graduates hired each year. It should therefore be one part of a broader retention strategy rather than a complete solution to turnover. Other retention options, such as wage increases, retention bonuses, and changes to working conditions, could potentially affect a larger share of departures. These alternatives are not computed here because comparable evidence linking both costs and turnover effects to a single hospital setting was not available.

## Conclusion

The first two links were not supported, but turnover remained costly, and the residency program generated a positive ROI under the published effect. Because that effect comes from a single study, the hospital should treat it as a best case rather than a guarantee. The ROI also counts only first-year savings. Silvestre et al. (2017) followed new graduate nurses for only 12 months and did not provide data that would allow year-two savings to be calculated. If retention benefits continue beyond the first year, the ROI estimates presented here may understate the program's true long-term value. The evidence therefore supports funding the program only if the hospital can expect to reach the break-even share.
