## Perfect Competition - Engagement Brief
This case is about choosing how many beds of tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun to plant in a 36 week season. The farm has 64 beds, but each crop has its own cap: 20 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds. All prices are set by the farmers’ market, so the farm is a price taker — it can’t choose what it earns, only how much it plants. Under perfect competition, the rule is to plant beds until the next bed’s marginal cost equals the market price (P = MC). The season length, the $20,000 fixed cost, the farmer’s 720 field hours, and the option to hire up to four workers with 1,440 hours each are all fixed. What limits the farm’s choice is labor: every bed requires weekly hours, and planting more beds of the same crop makes all of that crop’s beds harder to manage because of diminishing returns. The whole problem is deciding the mix of crops that fits inside those labor limits before marginal cost rises too high.
##
My hypothesis is 6 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 24 mesclun beds. Tomatoes get hard very fast because each extra bed adds a lot more labor, so I keep them small. Carrots stay easy even as you plant more, so I take the full 20 beds. Mesclun gets a little harder as it scales but not much, so I fill most of the remaining space with 24 beds. This mix fits inside the farm’s labor limits and stops each crop at the point where it makes sense to stop. If the model plants fewer than 20 carrot beds, then labor or diminishing returns must have stopped carrots earlier than I expected. If tomatoes end up far above 6 beds, then their marginal cost didn’t rise as quickly as I assumed. And if mesclun reaches all 30 beds, then labor clearly wasn’t as tight as I thought and mesclun stayed cheap enough to plant all the way to its cap.
